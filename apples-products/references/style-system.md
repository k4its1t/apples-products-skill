# English product-headline style system

Use this reference to generate and judge original copy. It is a synthesis of Apple’s current U.S. product pages, informed by but distinct from Apple’s historical product language. It is not an official Apple writing guide.

## Time and mode

Current product pages remain the default center of gravity. Historical Apple copy is useful because it exposes category creation, physical comparisons, compressed proof, and bolder declarative framing, but its surface language can date quickly.

- **Default / current:** favor human payoff, compact rhythm, and restrained play.
- **Legacy-aware:** borrow an older mechanism—such as a tangible comparison or category-defining proposition—while using current vocabulary and substantiation standards.
- **Period-specific:** reproduce the requested era’s level of formality, proof density, and boldness, but still write original copy and label the treatment as era-inspired when useful.

Do not blend eras indiscriminately. Early press-release superlatives, “revolutionary” language, and long specification subtitles should not leak into a contemporary hero unless the evidence and placement genuinely support them.

## The center of gravity

The characteristic effect comes from four things working together:

1. **Product truth first.** A visible design detail, capability, or use case supplies the idea.
2. **Human payoff second.** Technical facts become something a person can feel, do, or understand immediately.
3. **Maximum compression.** The line removes setup, qualifiers, and corporate framing.
4. **One clean turn.** A familiar phrase, contrast, sound pattern, or grammatical break adds memorability without obscuring meaning.

The copy is not merely short. It is short because the product image and support copy carry the explanation while the headline delivers the organizing thought.

## Voice and surface

- Use everyday English, concrete verbs, and familiar nouns.
- Sound assured rather than breathless. Most lines do not need an exclamation mark.
- Prefer sentence case. Preserve official capitalization for product and feature names.
- Let periods act as beats: one decisive sentence, two balanced fragments, or a short sequence.
- Use contractions when they make the line sound spoken and natural.
- Keep adjectives scarce. One exact adjective beats three promotional ones.
- Make wordplay discoverable on first reading. The reader should get the benefit before noticing the craft.

## Product-family tuning

### Entry and everyday products

Favor warmth, accessibility, motion, color, and light verbal play. Make capable feel friendly. Do not imply that an affordable product is compromised.

### Pro products

Favor authority, precision, performance, craft, and proof. Wordplay can be sharper, but it must not trivialize a serious workflow. Let specifications support the claim instead of cramming them into the headline.

### Wearables and health

Use active, encouraging language tied to movement, awareness, or connection. Avoid fear and clinical promises. Distinguish tracking or notification from diagnosis and treatment.

### Audio

Lean on sensory vocabulary, cadence, silence/sound contrast, and phrases that read well aloud. Avoid vague music metaphors that reveal no product benefit.

### Home

Center ease, atmosphere, togetherness, and unobtrusive intelligence. Make the technology feel present in the benefit and absent from the burden.

### Accessories

Turn a small physical action into a larger emotional or practical benefit. Utility puns work well when the object itself makes them inevitable.

### Spatial or emerging products

Express the new behavior before declaring a new era. Anchor wonder in something the user can picture doing.

## Reusable construction families

Use these as structures, never as fill-in-the-blank formulas.

### One-beat proposition

A compact phrase makes one product truth feel inevitable.

- Original example: `Pocket calm.`
- Best for: hero lines, accessories, a visually self-explanatory product.

### Balanced fragments

Two short units create contrast, cause and effect, or feature and value.

- Original example: `Less setup. More set free.`
- Best for: launches, upgrades, portability, speed.

### Rhythmic sequence

Three or four parallel beats map a simple journey or collection of actions.

- Original example: `Frame it. Fix it. Send it.`
- Best for: workflows and multi-use products.

### Familiar phrase, product-specific turn

An idiom changes at the one word only this product can justify.

- Original example for a compact projector: `A bright move.`
- Best for: memorable feature sections and accessible products.

### Scale contrast

Physical size, effort, time, or complexity contrasts with capability.

- Original example: `Tiny footprint. Room-filling ideas.`
- Best for: compact hardware and simplified tools.

### Technical noun, human consequence

A feature name or proof point is followed by what it changes for the user.

- Original example: `One sensor. Fewer second guesses.`
- Best for: pro, camera, health, and performance products.

### Update reveal

The familiar product or benefit is recast with a meaningful new capability.

- Original example: `Your daily drive. Now self-organizing.`
- Best for: new generations and software-enabled updates.

### Category claim with proof nearby

A bold claim leads only when the page can immediately substantiate it.

- Original example: `Our clearest view yet.`
- Best for: measurable advances. Replace or qualify the claim when proof is unavailable.

## Common failure modes

- **Apple-scented filler:** short fragments with no product truth underneath.
- **Pun first, meaning second:** the joke survives after the product is removed.
- **Adjective pile:** “beautiful, powerful, seamless, intelligent” substitutes for an idea.
- **Universal line:** it could advertise a phone, bank, shoe, and AI assistant unchanged.
- **Unearned superlative:** “best ever” appears without a defined measure and proof.
- **Famous-copy shadow:** the line recalls “Think different,” “There’s an app for that,” or a current headline closely enough to feel derivative.
- **Legacy cosplay:** period words and grand claims are copied at the surface without the product truth, launch context, or proof that made them work.
- **Period confetti:** every pair of words becomes a fragment even though the rhythm has no purpose.
- **Feature dump:** multiple specifications compete inside the headline.

## Final read-aloud check

Read the line once at natural speed. It should be clear without the visual, stronger with the visual, and easy to repeat from memory. If the turn calls attention to the copywriter before the product, simplify it.
